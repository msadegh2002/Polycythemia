# Erythrocytosis

<HematologyArt variant="erythrocyte" />

<div class="deck-content">

<p>Erythrocytosis means an increased red-cell concentration, usually recognized by an elevated hemoglobin or hematocrit. It may reflect a true increase in red-cell mass (absolute erythrocytosis) or a reduction in plasma volume (relative erythrocytosis).</p><p class="small">Clinical manifestations are often nonspecific and relate to impaired microvascular flow and blood hyperviscosity, with complications that include arterial and venous thrombosis, paradoxical bleeding, and progressive symptom burden.</p>

</div>

# Classification

<div class="deck-content">

<div class="classification-summary"><section><span class="num">1</span><h2>Relative(spurious)</h2><div><p>Plasma volume is reduced</p><p>Dehydration, diuretics, stress erythrocytosis or Gaisböck erythrocytosis</p></div></section><section><span class="num">2</span><h2>Absolute (true) / secondary</h2><div><p>Red-cell mass rises because EPO or hypoxia stimulates a normal clone</p><p>OSA(obstructive sleep apnea), hypoxia, smoking/carboxyhemoglobin, cardiopulmonary disease, high altitude, renal/hepatic tumors, exogenous EPO/androgens, and oxygen-sensing pathway disorders.</p></div></section><section><span class="num">3</span><h2>Absolute (true) / primary</h2><div><p>Red-cell mass rises because a marrow clone produces cells independently of EPO (low EPO levels)</p><p>PV (usually JAK2-driven), Primary familial and congenital polycythemia</p></div></section></div>

# Relative erythrocytosis: Gaisböck

<div class="deck-content">

<span class="section">PART 1</span><p class="subtitle">(stress or relative polycythemia)</p><p class="lead">The problem is concentration too little plasma around a near-normal red-cell mass</p><BloodVolumeScene compact fixed="relative" /><div class="columns small"><div><p>It is classically described in middle-aged men and is associated with obesity, cigarette smoking, hypertension, stress, and diuretic use.</p></div><div><p>The diagnosis is one of exclusion. Persistent erythrocytosis should first be distinguished from PV and secondary absolute erythrocytosis caused by hypoxia, erythropoietin-producing disorders, medications, or other underlying conditions.</p></div></div>

# Absolute secondary erythrocytosis: laboratory work-up

<div class="deck-content">

<p class="emphasis small">The laboratory goal is to prove an external driver and avoid mislabeling the patient as PV</p><dl class="rows compact"><div><dt>CBC trend</dt><dd>Confirm persistence; inspect WBC, platelets, MCV and smear.</dd></div><div><dt>Oxygen assessment</dt><dd>Pulse oximetry, sleep study, smoking/CO history, pulmonary and cardiac evaluation when indicated.</dd></div><div><dt>Serum EPO</dt><dd>Usually normal or high in secondary disease; interpret “normal” relative to the degree of erythrocytosis.</dd></div><div><dt>JAK2 testing</dt><dd>Usually absent; if low EPO or the phenotype is strongly suggestive, test JAK2 before closing the case.</dd></div><div><dt>Targeted tests</dt><dd>Medication review, renal/hepatic evaluation, imaging or hereditary work-up according to the history.</dd></div></dl>

# PV: Pathophysiology

<div class="deck-content">

<div class="columns small"><div><h2>High EPO Levels:</h2><p>Cellular hypoxia can occur due to any causes that triggers the release of erythropoietin from the renal peritubular interstitial fibroblast-like cells. A small amount of EPO is also produced by the liver. Erythropoietin then acts on erythroid progenitor cells, stimulating erythropoiesis.</p></div><div><h2>Low EPO Levels:</h2><p>The primary defect in nearly 95% of cases of polycythemia vera is an acquired mutation in exon 14 of the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) gene (V617F). Mutations have also been described in exon 12 of the JAK2 gene. These mutations disrupt the inhibitory regulation of the pseudo kinase domain, leading to constitutive signaling. This constitutive activation results in both hypersensitivity to EPO and EPO-independent erythroid colony formation.</p></div></div>

</div>

# PV: Epidemiology

<div class="deck-content">

<div class="epidemiology"><div><div class="stat">22</div><p class="stat-caption">The prevalence of polycythemia vera is approximately 22 cases per 100,000 population.</p></div><div><ul><li>No consistent or clinically meaningful differences in incidence have been demonstrated across racial or ethnic groups.</li><li>Polycythemia vera shows a slight male predominance (male-to-female ratio approximately 1.2 to 1.5:1).</li><li>Polycythemia vera is most commonly diagnosed after age 50, but younger adults may be affected.</li><li>In contrast, polycythemia due to hemoglobinopathies and congenital cyanotic heart diseases is usually detected in younger patients.</li></ul></div></div>

# PV Complications

<HematologyArt variant="marrow" />

<div class="deck-content">

<div class="complications-prose"><p>This condition can be associated with over production of all 3 cell lines, with a notable predilection for red blood cells.</p><p>The manifestations of erythrocytosis, regardless of cause, typically stem from the associated risks of blood hyperviscosity, including thrombotic events.</p><p>Paradoxically, bleeding events are also frequently reported.</p><p>Additionally, the rare risk of leukemic transformation, particularly after long-term cytotoxic therapy, may influence management.</p></div>

</div>

# Clinical phenotype: PV complications and risk

<div class="deck-content">

<dl class="rows compact wide-label"><div><dt>Thrombosis</dt><dd>Arterial, venous or unusual-site events; the major preventable complication.</dd></div><div><dt>Microvascular symptoms</dt><dd>Headache, visual disturbance, erythromelalgia and acral paresthesia.</dd></div><div><dt>Bleeding</dt><dd>Especially with extreme thrombocytosis and acquired von Willebrand syndrome.</dd></div><div><dt>Conventional high risk</dt><dd>Age ≥60 years or previous thrombosis.</dd></div><div><dt>Progression</dt><dd>Post-PV myelofibrosis or blast phase: cytopenias, rising LDH, splenomegaly or blasts.</dd></div></dl>
